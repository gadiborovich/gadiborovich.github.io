AI agents can do the work, not just help someone do it.The New Stack and the New Company develop together.

For most of the SaaS era, a good product helped someone do a hard job; the person still finished it. Bookkeeping software organized the records; a finance team still closed the books. A security product flagged an issue; someone still investigated it and made the fix. fix.

By an agent, we mean software you can hand a job to. It works through the steps and comes back with the job done. The promise to the customer becomes more direct. I do not want better bookkeeping software; I want the books closed. I do not want another security dashboard; I want the system tested, the obvious issues fixed, and the real exceptions put in front of the right person. Doing this reliably inside a company that can work through the steps of a job. Making that dependable inside a business is much harder than making a good demo. The agent needs the right convincing demo. The product needs context and permissions. It has to remember what happened, recover when something fails, know when to ask for help, and leave a record a person can inspect. The model may get a customer interested. The system around it determines whether that customer will trust it on a Tuesday afternoon when money, data, or a customer relationship is at stake. One way to see the progress is to ask how large a task an agent can finish on its own. METR, a nonprofit that evaluates frontier AI systems, grades technical tasks by how long they take a human expert and tracks which of them agents complete reliably. Each model generation handles longer tasks than the one before, and the growth has been exponential. Anthropic sees the same change in use: more Claude Code and Cowork sessions now consist of long-running agentic tasks rather than conversations. The tasks METR studies are cleaner than most jobs inside a company, but agents can handle longer sequences than they could even a year ago.[1]

Agents make it possible to build software businesses around jobs that were too manual, messy, or expensive for the last generation to automate. That is where we see the opportunity. inspect.

The New Company is a business designed around agents doing part or all of a job. Once that becomes possible, an industry can revisit its basic assumptions: what the product is, how the company operates, what it costs to serve a customer, how the customer pays, and how large the market can become. The business model can change with the product. In April 2026, HubSpot began charging fifty cents each time its customer agent resolved a conversation.[2] The important change is the unit: an established software company charging for a completed job instead of access to a tool. The New Stack and the New Company advance together: taking on a customer's work with those capabilities. Its product, operations, cost to serve, and way of charging the customer can all change.

The two sides develop together. More ambitious products expose what the infrastructure still lacks, and better infrastructure makes more ambitious products possible. What Antigravity is

Antigravity Capital is a seed firm we started in 2022. We invest at pre-seed and seed, while the product, business, and first team are still taking shape. We can see how a founder thinks and builds, but many of the decisions that will define the company are still ahead. This is the part of company building we know best and where we think an investor can help most.

We look for founders who know how to build with AI agents: what they can do, where they fail, and how they change both the product and the company. We invest in the agentic software stack that makes agents dependable and in the companies designed around what agents can now do.

Much of our access begins before there is a round, sometimes before a builder has decided what to start or join. We introduce engineers to young companies and founders to early teammates or customers. Those relationships have led to first checks, early hires across the portfolio, and room in competitive rounds.

Daniel cofounded Disqus out of Y Combinator in 2007 and ran it as CEO for ten years, from the first version of the product through a successful acquisition by Zeta Global. Disqus ran comment sections on over four million websites. If you read or wrote a comment under an article in the 2010s, there is a decent chance it was powered by Disqus. After the acquisition, Daniel began advising founders and investing in young companies.

Gadi came to San Francisco from Uruguay, joined the equity crowdfunding platform Wefunder early, and later started XX, its accelerator and early-stage fund. Daniel first advised XX companies, then joined Gadi for a time as an investment partner. At XX, we found that we cared about the same stage of company building and made better decisions together. Both of us now make every Antigravity investment decision and work directly with founders.

The name comes from an analogy we use with founders: the market is like gravity. It is real, and it does not care about your plans. You cannot ignore gravity, but you can build an airplane.

What we believe

AI agents can do the work, not just help someone do it.

For most of the SaaS era, a good product helped someone do a hard job; the person still finished it. Bookkeeping software organized the records; a finance team still closed the books. A security product flagged an issue; someone still investigated it and made the fix. By an agent, we mean software you can hand a job to. It works through the steps and comes back with the job done. The promise to the customer becomes more direct. I do not want better bookkeeping software; I want the books closed. I do not want another security dashboard; I want the system tested, the obvious issues fixed, and the real exceptions put in front of the right person. Doing this reliably inside a company is much harder than making a good demo. The agent needs the right context and permissions. It has to remember what happened, recover when something fails, know when to ask for help, and leave a record a person can inspect. The model may get a customer interested. The system around it determines whether that customer will trust it on a Tuesday afternoon when money, data, or a customer relationship is at stake. One way to see the progress is to ask how large a task an agent can finish on its own. METR, a nonprofit that evaluates frontier AI systems, grades technical tasks by how long they take a human expert and tracks which of them agents complete reliably. Each model generation handles longer tasks than the one before, and the growth has been exponential. Anthropic sees the same change in use: more Claude Code and Cowork sessions now consist of long-running agentic tasks rather than conversations. The tasks METR studies are cleaner than most jobs inside a company, but agents can handle longer sequences than they could even a year ago.[1]

Agents make it possible to build software businesses around jobs that were too manual, messy, or expensive for the last generation to automate. That is where we see the opportunity. We use two connected ideas to describe it. The New Stack is the evaluation, memory, permissions, security, and other machinery that turns a convincing demo into something a customer can trust with real work. The New Company is a business designed around agents doing part or all of a job. Once that becomes possible, an industry can revisit its basic assumptions: what the product is, how the company operates, what it costs to serve a customer, how the customer pays, and how large the market can become. The business model can change with the product. In April 2026, HubSpot began charging fifty cents each time its customer agent resolved a conversation.[2] The important change is the unit: an established software company charging for a completed job instead of access to a tool. The New Stack and the New Company advance together: ambitious products expose what the infrastructure still lacks, and better infrastructure makes more ambitious products possible. The internet connected software to people and businesses around the world. Agents expand the work software can carry out on their behalf. We believe this will be an even larger change.

A useful AI product can still be easy to replace.

Software is getting easier to build. Models improve, obvious features get copied, incumbents bundle them, and customers can build more for themselves. A product can be useful and grow quickly while the company behind it remains easy to replace. A lasting company needs something a competitor cannot copy as quickly as a feature. Trust and permission deepen as a product does more work. A system of record becomes harder to leave. Feedback from doing the job can improve the product. An unusual way to reach customers can be just as important. Often it starts with one narrow job whose result the customer can judge. Do it well and the customer will share more context, allow more access, and hand over more work. We look for products where success at the first job makes the next one easier to earn.

As building gets easier, judgment matters more.

A few strong people can now test an idea, build a product, and reach customers faster than a much larger team could a few years ago. More good ideas can become companies. Markets can also fill quickly with polished products that have no clear reason to exist. The tools increase what a small team can make. They do not decide what is worth making. Founders still have to choose the problem, notice when the evidence points somewhere else, and know which parts of the product have to be unusually good. Agents also let people contribute beyond the specialty they started with. Technical founders can handle more of their own selling and customer conversations. People close to customers can build and test a product before a larger team exists. A founding team needs fewer specialists before it can move. That makes the first few hires unusually important. Each person has more leverage, and the early technical hires need range as well as depth. Hiring is one of the first places a founder's judgment becomes visible.

Building with agents changes who can start a company.

Building well with agents requires skills that barely existed a few years ago. A strong builder can take an ambiguous goal, decide what an agent should do and when a person should step in, and understand what to change when the system fails. They can explain why something works and see where a new capability could become a product or company. Schools, titles, past employers, and familiar networks still tell us something. They do not yet reliably show who can build this way. Someone can be unusually good at it and look ordinary on paper. So we look at what someone has actually made: a shipped product, real users or revenue, or even a rough tool that solved a real problem. We ask what they chose to automate, what they checked themselves, and what changed when the first attempt failed. Builders like this exist far outside the usual venture networks. What they make gives us a way to recognize them before their credentials do.

Someone has built something interesting and wants an honest read. We make the introduction, say what we think, and stay close. If a company emerges, we have already seen how the founder builds, and the founder knows whether we are useful.

One recent example is Terac, an Antigravity portfolio company that recruits and manages participants for AI research teams. We met its founder, Jack Blair, in 2024, when he was building a different company and had no reason to pitch us. We stayed close as he started Terac. When Terac raised its seed, the round was competitive. The founders of two companies we had already backed, Flai and Bem, told Jack what we had been like to work with and helped us earn room. Jack chose us with that history; we invested from a view formed before the fundraise began.

We choose the questions, read the evidence, argue with each other, and decide.

AFK Frontier models bring broad intelligence and research ability. They do not arrive knowing Antigravity. AFK, short for Antigravity Firm Knowledge, is the system we are building to make our experience useful to them. We want that history to shape the next investigation. An agent could recognize a problem we have seen in another industry, bring back a concern we left unresolved, or challenge us with a company we misjudged. Each new investment, pass, and founder conversation gives us more to learn from. We are building AFK so that experience helps us ask better questions, revisit old assumptions, and make better decisions. Daniel and Gadi make every investment decision.

What Fund I showed us

We began investing Fund I in 2023. The $2.1 million fund has backed 21 companies, with about $1.85 million invested as of September, 2026. It is still a young portfolio. The company progress is encouraging; the eventual investment results remain largely ahead of us.[1]

Flai makes the New Company idea tangible. It entered dealerships through voice AI and communication, then expanded into scheduling and CRM. It aims to own the software that organizes the work and increasingly perform the coordination people used to do inside it. Toyota's US headquarters is now a customer and distribution partner. In August, Flai reported about $350,000 in live monthly recurring revenue and $5.7 million in contracted annual recurring revenue, which includes pilots. It has closed a $27 million Series A at $150 million post-money.[1]

Bem shows how a new relationship can develop through useful work. Gadi first saw Antonio Bustamante present at an AI demo night hosted by Guillermo Rauch, when he was not fundraising. We stayed in touch and introduced engineers and potential customers. We later wrote the first check in a new round, helped bring a lead term sheet, and introduced angels. Antonio had experienced how we worked and made room for a larger investment.[5] Bem has since closed a $10 million Series A at $50 million post-money.[6]

Pace shows the work of finding a direction when the original one is not working. It began as a consumer product. Daniel has spoken with Victoria roughly every week or two since we invested, working through the product direction and how to bring it to market. Pace now sells AI tutoring through education institutions and content providers, helping adult learners prepare for qualifications and jobs, including people who struggle with English. It powers the tutor in the official GED & Me app and is working with GED and MedCerts on healthcare training. The company's progress reflects Victoria's technical ability and the credibility she has earned in a difficult market.[1]

Halluminate is part of the New Stack: it builds training environments and datasets that help frontier AI labs teach agents to use software and perform knowledge work. As the labs push into more complex tasks, they need realistic environments and examples of how that work gets done. Halluminate is now at a $100 million annualized revenue run rate, with Anthropic as its largest customer, and has closed a $30 million Series A at $200 million post-money. We continue helping it meet engineers through Puentes as the team grows.

Shopgenie built an AI service advisor for auto repair shops; we invested early and helped the founders test whether shops would buy the product. It became Fund I's first realized exit, returning approximately 4.44 times our invested cost before fund-level fees and carry. We distributed $140,000 to LPs and retained the remaining proceeds for reinvestment; some have already gone back into companies. [1][5]

Fund I also helped us learn we care about ownership. In some of the companies where our conviction has grown through working with the founders, our position is smaller than we would like. Fund II is designed to let us take more meaningful initial positions while keeping the portfolio focused enough to do this work.

How we work

Antigravity is two partners, and we intend to keep it that way. We both make every investment decision. Our discussions connect what we have seen in the founder with what we understand about the opportunity.

An investment can begin with excitement about someone one of us wants to work with. Then we examine whether that conviction holds under scrutiny. What do we understand? Which parts do not make sense yet? Sometimes one of us has seen something in a founder that we have not explained well. Sometimes a question exposes how little we understand about the business. The conversation can lead us to pass, or to understand the uncertainty better and decide to back the team.

AI has made that process richer. Daniel has spent considerable time building the tools we use to research unfamiliar markets, investigate the strongest objections, and return to what a founder actually told us. We can divide a review among agents investigating the product, customers, market, economics, and the case against investing. We choose the questions, read the evidence, challenge it, and decide.

Memory is especially valuable. AFK, short for Antigravity Firm Knowledge, is the system we are building to connect founder conversations, company updates, research, and past decisions. We want to preserve what we believed at the time, where we disagreed, what changed our minds, and what we never resolved. That history can help the next investigation begin with better questions instead of starting over.

We are still learning how to use these tools well. Producing more research is useful only if we absorb it and it makes the decision clearer. Our aim is for both partners to understand why we want to make an investment, what could invalidate the case, and which risks we are choosing to take.

What Fund II is

Fund II is a $10 million target fund. Over a three-year investment period, we plan to make approximately 25 initial investments at pre-seed and seed. We expect initial checks of $250,000 to $500,000, averaging about $300,000, with typical entry ownership of 1% to 2%.[4]

The strategy continues the work of Fund I. The main change is ownership: we want to own more of the companies where we have conviction and can make a meaningful contribution. More capital allows larger initial checks without a large increase in the number of companies we back.

That portfolio size also reflects partner time. Knowing people before a round, forming an independent view, and staying close through difficult decisions all require attention. We want both partners to understand why each investment was made and to remain useful as the company develops.

At a $10 million fund size, a meaningful stake in an exceptional company can matter to the whole fund. We are building the firm around knowing the people early, earning the opportunity to invest, and having enough ownership for the work and judgment to matter.
