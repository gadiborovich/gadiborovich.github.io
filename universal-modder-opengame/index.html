Investment field guide · October 14, 2026

Who becomes the home
for AI-made games?

Universal Modder on its own merits, through the lens of what we learned—and actually decided—about OpenGame.

A layered read: follow the chapters for the argument; open the deeper notes for the mechanics. Interactive creator examples and ownership math are included.

My present read: Universal Modder has a credible way into an emerging creator workflow. The unresolved question is whether it can turn talented makers and striking demos into a service people repeatedly need, and eventually a destination players choose. That deserves focused diligence. The evidence does not yet make the discussed $78–80M entry price persuasive on its own.

OpenGame sharpens that question. It does not answer it. We wanted to invest there because creating and tinkering could itself be valuable, with hosting and game-specific services as the first product. Missing the allocation gives us useful learning—not a reason to buy the next company in the category.

This is an unlisted, publicly accessible reading page, not a private data room. Raw archives, messages, transcripts and reference material remain local. Facts, founder claims, plans and investment judgments are distinguished throughout.

01 / Reset the comparison

We decided to invest in OpenGame.

The right starting point is the final decision, not the earlier list of objections. On September 25, Daniel wrote: “Count Antigravity in for $200k on that SAFE.” The preceding terms email specified an $18M cap on a standard YC SAFE. On October 8, Matt said the round could not accommodate us after larger checks and the dilution constraint. That is an allocation outcome, not a reversal of our investment judgment. [Decision record]

“Creating and tinkering can be the appeal, even if only a few friends ever play it.”

Daniel’s September 25 investment email

The same email favored creators making ambitious games and interactive experiences, beginning with GitHub/Vercel-like hosting and sharing, then an opinionated creator stack. It explicitly did not rely on a consumer discovery feed as the starting point. The underwriting was about a new creative behavior becoming possible, and a team positioned to serve it.

That matters because a game-making product can succeed before its customers become successful game studios. Someone can pay to make a strange racing game for four friends, just as someone can pay for a creative tool without building a media business. If we demand a mass player audience immediately from Universal Modder, we have silently changed the thesis. If Universal Modder itself chooses free creator tools and future audience monetization, however, that choice makes player demand more central to its own business.

The comparison has three separate questions

Category: does cheaper game creation create a valuable new behavior? Company: is this team’s product the place that behavior accumulates? Investment: do the ownership, price and potential outcomes make the risk attractive? An affirmative answer to the first is insufficient for the other two.

Apply the same evidentiary standard to both. OpenGame’s September operating evidence also did not establish product–market fit. Our decision was a forward-looking team and market bet. Universal Modder can earn such a bet, but must earn it independently.

Time boundary: OpenGame’s detailed operating baseline comes from the September 17 ZIP, September meetings and subsequent notes. Its financing history was refreshed through October 8. Universal Modder’s materials are from October 14. Current competitor documentation was checked for this guide. These are not synchronized product audits.

02 / Learn the stack

A game is much more than generated code.

Imagine making a small cooperative haunted-house game. A friend should open a link, join you, pick up a key, escape a monster and come back tomorrow with their progress intact. “AI made the game” compresses many jobs into one phrase.

MakeAgent · code · assets
RunEngine · rules · controls
PublishBuild · host · update
Play togetherIdentity · rooms · state
ReturnFun · friends · discovery

The agent writes. The harness helps it work.

A coding agent can edit files, use tools and respond to failures. A harness is the surrounding workflow: instructions, available tools, project context, execution, tests and feedback. A good game harness might inspect a screenshot, recognize a collision failure, change code and test again. A prompt box is only the visible interface. The useful question is whether the surrounding system produces a working result with less effort and fewer failed iterations.

Universal Modder starts with makers’ existing agents and supplies reusable know-how and tools. OpenGame’s plan included both external-agent publishing and an integrated studio. Those are different starting emphases, not mutually exclusive architectures. A platform can support an external agent while still requiring its own game runtime.

The engine runs the game after the agent stops.

The engine or runtime repeatedly updates the world: input, movement, collisions, rules, sound and rendering. Three.js supplies browser 3D rendering; a creator still needs game behavior and supporting systems. A fuller engine bundles more of those systems and conventions. These pitches primarily concern agents generating conventional code and assets that then run as games. Do not confuse that with a model generating every video frame live.

A familiar web stack is often easier for coding agents to inspect and edit. A specialized engine can provide more reliable built-in behavior, at the cost of learning its abstractions and accepting its constraints. Neither “web” nor “real engine” settles game quality. Browser delivery can reduce installation friction; it does not guarantee fast loading, good mobile controls or stable performance.

Why engine choice changes the business

An engine choice determines which game ideas are easy, which assets import cleanly, how debugging works and where the finished result can run. Supporting many engines expands supply but multiplies deployment and support cases. Supporting one well can improve reliability while limiting what creators can build.

Web delivery is also not unique to Three.js: Godot supports browser exports, with documented performance, threading and networking constraints. That is why “uses Godot” and “plays in a browser” are not opposites. Godot’s export documentation illustrates the tradeoffs; it does not establish any startup’s implementation quality.

Assets are a production pipeline, not a single model call.

Our monster needs a model, a surface appearance, an animation skeleton, movements, collision geometry and the right scale. Rigging adds the skeleton; retargeting transfers an existing motion onto a compatible character. The result also has to load quickly and fit the game’s visual style. Better asset models help everyone; reliable assembly is a separate job.

Universal Modder’s demo discussion is useful here: the team describes combining generated assets, Blender workflows and existing animations rather than generating every element from scratch. That is practical workflow knowledge. It is not evidence of an exclusive asset advantage. The transcript calls Mixamo “open source”; Adobe describes a licensed, royalty-free service with usage conditions. Those terms are different. [UM demo] [Adobe FAQ]

Publishing is the boundary between “works for me” and a product.

The build process packages the game and its dependencies. Hosting delivers it. Versioning lets the creator update it without breaking the version friends are playing. Diagnostics explain crashes. For an installed mod, the platform must also handle the player’s game version and local files. The test is not whether a founder can launch a demo on their laptop; it is whether a stranger can publish and another stranger can play.

Multiplayer is a service promise.

A lobby helps friends meet. Shared game state makes them see the same monster and key. Persistence remembers progress. Competitive games may need stronger server authority to resist cheating; cooperative experiments may tolerate simpler designs. Voice chat, moderation, reconnection and regional latency add work. “Multiplayer by default” is attractive precisely because integrating and operating these pieces well is difficult. Ask what works now, under which conditions, and who pays when play time grows.

The potential company lives in the joins: an agent makes a change, the assets still work, the build ships, friends join the same version, errors reach the creator, and the next revision is better. A list of integrations does not prove that whole path.

03 / The customer determines the product

“Anyone can make a game” hides several customers.

A beginner wants a first satisfying result. A committed hobbyist wants control and repeatability. A modder wants to change a world they already love. A professional studio may want production tooling without adopting a new distribution platform. A product designed for one can frustrate another.

A first game without a development setup

A new creator needs defaults that make the first result achievable. Compare the intervention required to make a second, specific change—not just the quality of the first generation.

Rehan describes the initial Universal Modder customer as the most capable prosumers: people who already have their agents, preferences and working methods. That is a plausible entry point because the team can recruit people like itself. It also creates a demanding retention test: these users have the ability to build around a weak platform.

OpenGame’s September sequence overlapped: host work made with external agents, then offer a studio for people who want models, assets and creative defaults together. Its intended audience was not limited to professional studios. Universal Modder’s demo discussion also leaves room for an internal agent later. Treat those sequences as hypotheses about where to start, not permanent philosophical identities. [OpenGame packet] [UM meetings]

What to observe: give each creator a meaningful second request. Change the monster’s behavior, recover a broken revision, add a friend, move the project elsewhere. The gap between a polished first generation and controlled iteration reveals the product’s actual customer.

04 / Universal Modder independently

One launcher, three operating problems.

Universal Modder wants original games and modifications of existing games to share a home. The intuition is appealing: people who love established worlds and people making the next interesting world may overlap. But putting them in the same catalogue does not make the operational requirements equal.

The three content lanes
LaneWhat makes it attractiveWhat must work
Original browser gamesA link can get a friend into a new experience quickly. The creator controls the design.Quality, load time, controls, multiplayer and repeat appeal. Each asset still needs appropriate provenance.
Traditional installed modsServe demand around a game people already own and enjoy.Supported versions, dependencies, safe installation, updates, publisher rules and recovery when the underlying game changes.
Local-asset reconstructions and two-game mashupsMake surprising combinations possible; recognizable worlds attract attention.Exact prerequisites and format-specific permissions. Some demos reuse local assets in a different runtime; others run two games together. Those are distinct technical cases.

The third lane is especially easy to misunderstand. A browser-like scene using local game assets is not necessarily a mod running inside the original engine. A two-game pass-through is not necessarily that scene either. In the demo, the founders discuss both. Any operational or rights conclusion must identify which technique a particular title uses.

That breadth could be an advantage if the common publishing layer works across it. It could also consume the team: every new game engine, operating system, version and asset format adds failure modes. The first strategic choice is which path becomes dependable enough for independent users before the catalogue expands.

  • Hollow Crown: Rehan’s browser game and public source. The older Shadow Strike URL redirects to it; count one project, not two.
  • Don’t Look Away / Weeping Angels: a reachable horror demo associated with Blendi. A demo is not automatically a durable game or a platform launch.
  • Installed-game work: examples and field notes in the pinned Universal Modder repository, plus public mod demonstrations. Source examples, a public clip and a tested downloadable release are different evidence.

The packet verified page reachability and source artifacts, not gameplay quality or clean-machine installation. It also does not establish that every associated founder project belongs to the new company. Some viral examples in the wider wave were made by outside creators. They validate interest in a behavior, not exclusive company execution. [Artifact checks]

Independent thesis: help strong makers bring whatever tools they prefer, turn their experiments into dependable experiences, and give those experiences a reason to remain on the platform. The first step has concrete evidence. The full sequence remains to be demonstrated.

05 / Take the competitors seriously

Spawn and Astrocade expose the real choices.

The company referred to as “Astrogate” is Astrocade in both source sets. The relevant Spawn is spawn.co. OpenGame’s September competition deep dive also identifies Tesana as a close competitor. Reading their current primary documentation changes several of the founders’ comparisons.

Three corrections before comparing

Spawn supports outside coding agents. Tesana now documents Three.js/Phaser for new games, rather than being Godot-only. Astrocade targets casual play, but its own teaching emphasizes repeated play and progression. “Closed agent,” “different engine” and “disposable games” are therefore insufficient summaries. These are current documented offers, not hands-on quality assessments. [Competitor sources]

Spawn: accept one runtime, get an integrated world.

Spawn’s default collaborator is Savi, but it documents connecting an external coding agent and working on the same game files. Its September 5 version-six release also describes collaborative building, persistent worlds and integrated physics. It is making a substantive runtime bet, not merely putting a prompt box in front of a generic model. [FAQ] [Version six]

The important boundary is migration. Spawn’s import guide accepts existing source, including projects from familiar web and game engines, but adapts the game to Spawn. The old engine, server, database and networking do not simply come along. In exchange, the platform offers common infrastructure. Importing a game therefore differs from hosting its unchanged build, and from modifying a commercial PC game in its native engine. [Import guide]

The investment implication: Universal Modder needs to show that preserving a maker’s engine, local workflow or existing-game community is more valuable than the integration gained by moving to Spawn. “We let you use your own agent” is no longer a sufficient distinction. OpenGame’s external-agent hosting thesis faces the same competitive pressure.

Source access is not the same as a portable game

Spawn documents a local source copy, but not a ready-to-run standalone export. The copy need not include all assets, and platform-dependent behavior needs adaptation to leave. Its “mods” are reusable tools or content inside Spawn, not a claim to support every existing PC game. [Spawn FAQ]

That is a useful tradeoff rather than an automatic defect. A creator might gladly accept dependence if shared play, operations and audience are excellent. The diligence task is to test both the benefit and the switching cost.

Astrocade: shorten the distance from an idea to a moment of fun.

Astrocade’s launch described an orchestration model, AstroBrain. Its later agentic product announcement described specialist agents and game-specific editing interfaces. An orchestration model coordinates creation; that does not establish a proprietary game engine. The reviewed primary materials do not give a sufficiently clear runtime specification to label it equivalent to Spawn. [Launch] [Agentic product]

Its Academy offers a clearer customer definition: mobile-friendly, casual experiences, short sessions, and roughly 5–15 seconds to the first enjoyable interaction, including loading. It also teaches replayability and progression. A game can be simple to enter and still be worth returning to. Calling these games “shallow” misses a deliberate design constraint. [Academy]

The creator proposition includes publishing, discovery, analytics and earnings tied to plays. That makes Astrocade relevant to Universal Modder’s distribution-and-payout ambition, even if the initial creators and formats differ. The current payout formula, export contract and external-agent route were not established by the primary pages reviewed. Treat those as gaps, not categorical absences. [Creator hub]

The investment implication: a more technically elaborate demo does not necessarily beat Astrocade for the casual player’s job. For Universal Modder, ask whether people want a surprising short experience, a deeper game with friends, or a modification of a game they already play. Those can require different distribution and retention strategies.

Tesana: the old architecture distinction has moved.

OpenGame’s September documents described Tesana as Godot-specific. Current Tesana documentation describes TypeScript/Vite projects, Three.js for 3D and Phaser for 2D, with older Godot projects supported. It documents a build-and-check loop that runs the game in a browser and repairs errors. The precise transition date was not established. The competitive point today is that a standard web stack and integrated agent are available together. [Architecture]

Tesana’s paid plans support standalone web/desktop exports. Full source export is limited to original projects; copies, template-derived games and earlier remixes can still export playable builds. Its hosted online services can still matter after export. Portability is more concrete here than a vague claim that creators “own their games,” though delivery quality remains untested. [Exports] [Multiplayer]

A small but revealing policy change: Tesana says remixing other creators’ games ended October 9; creators can still copy their own games. “Remix” is not a fixed category attribute. It is a product and rights choice that can change. [Copy policy]

Five meanings of “open” to ask about separately
  1. Agent choice: can I keep the coding tool I prefer?
  2. Source access: can I inspect, edit and retain the files?
  3. Runtime choice: can the project keep its existing engine?
  4. Operational portability: do assets, saves and multiplayer work after I leave?
  5. Commercial freedom: can I distribute and monetize elsewhere, and under what terms?

Universal Modder’s public toolkit provides concrete evidence for the first two. It does not by itself answer all five for the future hosted platform. OpenGame welcomed external creation, but its September materials also did not settle every export and licensing term. The right comparison is a checklist of dependencies, not one open/closed label.

The competitor is sometimes the existing workflow.

For an expert, the alternative may be an ordinary agent, public game libraries, asset tools, a repository, a hosting service and a community chat. This route is fragmented but flexible. Universal Modder and OpenGame must remove enough integration work or supply enough audience to justify another platform. Spawn tries to remove that fragmentation by standardizing the environment. Astrocade reduces the scope of the first creation and the first play. Tesana combines an integrated creation path with more explicit export options.

These companies can converge. An integrated studio can admit external agents; an open toolkit can add an internal studio. The durable comparison is therefore the behavior and dependencies each company wins, not a screenshot of today’s interface.

The cost structure follows the product choice.

An external-agent creator may pay their own generation bill, which can reduce Universal Modder’s early subsidy. The company still carries builds, bandwidth, storage, multiplayer, support and content operations. An integrated studio absorbs more of the creation journey and can charge for it; it also pays for unsuccessful iterations. The right denominator is the cost of a successful, repeatable creator outcome—not the cost of one prompt.

OpenGame proposed creator subscriptions/credits around $20–$200 per month and a 20% marketplace share. Its plan to subsidize early creation was a spending assumption, not demonstrated unit economics. Tesana currently lists $25/$50/$200 monthly creator tiers and a 30% on-platform sales share, with no off-platform revenue share. That is a concrete competing way to capture creative-tool value. [OpenGame memo] [Tesana plans] [Tesana sales]

Spawn’s strategy essay argues for subsidizing creation so metering does not interrupt experimentation. Its product page describes a 50/50 player-spend split, while its FAQ says paid access and recurring subscriptions are not currently available to creators. Those may be different product scopes or documentation drift; they do not establish mature monetization. Astrocade advertises creator earnings based on plays, but the current formula and sustainable margin were not verified. [Spawn strategy] [Product] [FAQ] [Astrocade creators]

Free creation can maximize experimentation. It also increases the importance of knowing where revenue eventually comes from. Creator payouts are an expense or a share of revenue; they are not themselves the source of that revenue.

The model suppliers are both accelerants and dependencies.

Improving general agents and asset models expand what these products can do. They can also erase an orchestration advantage or make a once-special workflow routine. Existing subscriptions may subsidize experimentation today without guaranteeing the same cost structure later. Ask which improvement benefits the startup, which removes its value, and whether creators stay when their base agent becomes better at deployment and debugging.

Astrocade is also pursuing a different claimed defense: an “Experience Model” informed by creator requests and player interactions. That is a potential learning advantage from operating the audience, beyond assembling third-party tools. Its architecture and performance advantage were not independently established here. [Astrocade’s current positioning]

A convincing answer names a continuing job—operating a shared world, handling compatibility, delivering a relevant audience, or maintaining a reliable release path. “We connect the best models” is a starting capability, not a complete defense.

07 / Where value accumulates

Making, playing and discovering are different loops.

The maker loop

Build → publish → feedback → improve → publish again.

Value can come from enjoyment, creative control, less debugging or easier sharing. A subscription can work even if only a few friends play.

The player loop

Discover → launch successfully → enjoy → return or invite a friend.

Value comes from the experience, not the method used to create it. A player need not care that AI was involved.

Cheap generation accelerates the beginning of the maker loop. It does not automatically improve the rest. A catalogue can fill with unfinished experiments, each played once by its creator. That could still support a useful creative tool; it would not yet establish an entertainment network.

A network begins to show itself when a creator gains players they did not bring, uses their feedback, and stays because that audience or service is hard to reproduce elsewhere. The reciprocal observation is a player returning and choosing a second creator’s work. A founder’s hit hosted on the company’s domain is weaker evidence: it might be a successful game without a successful platform.

Distribution is not just “we have followers.”

Followers and clips can produce the first visit. Curated shelves, social invitations, recommendations and reliable play can produce the second. An established game’s recognizable characters can attract a click without making an unfamiliar platform valuable. The acquisition question is therefore format-specific: which audiences arrive for mods, which arrive for original games, and how often do they cross?

Universal Modder’s founders emphasize distribution and eventual creator payouts. Gadi emphasized dependable hosting and game services. These can reinforce each other, but they are different initial promises. If creators arrive for an audience, the platform needs players. If they arrive to ship reliable multiplayer, a smaller user base can already create value. The first roadmap and pricing experiments should reveal which promise the company is testing. [Business meeting]

What could become defensible?

A public knowledge base can attract contributors and improve everyone’s workflow. Its openness is not a proprietary data moat. More durable advantages could come from reliable operations, creator relationships, trusted identity and payment systems, player friendships, useful discovery, or publisher cooperation. These have to accumulate through use; they do not exist because the deck draws a flywheel.

The best question for “GitHub/Vercel for games” is specific: what valuable work does the platform repeatedly do that the creator would otherwise have to do—and what gets better because other people are already there? File hosting alone gives a narrow answer. Shared game services and a genuine audience could give a much larger one.

10 / What exactly would we own?

Company title and content rights are separate gates.

1. The company’s own assets

Universal Modder’s October 14 follow-up says early work was connected to fal and releases to the proposed new company were being arranged. Rehan explicitly said investment would close once this was resolved. The packet contains no executed release, assignments, incorporation record or cap table.

A rebuild and rebrand was described as a fallback. That is a possible path, not proof the current code, brand, audience relationships and timetable transfer unchanged.

2. The games and assets it hosts

Separately, each format needs an appropriate route for code, artwork, local assets, modifications and monetization. A player having a legitimate game copy does not settle every downstream use.

Original games, sanctioned mods, local reconstructions and two-game mashups should have distinct treatment rather than one blanket “UGC” explanation.

The public repository’s rules against shipping game files, decompiled dumps or bypassing DRM/anti-cheat are meaningful design intentions. They do not verify the complete publication pipeline or resolve every publisher’s terms. Steam’s agreement itself distinguishes licensed access from title to software and recognizes additional terms. The practical request is a format-by-format review with counsel and a demonstration of the actual controls—not a generic declaration that mods are legal or illegal. [Steam agreement] [Repository]

OpenGame also used recognizable third-party IP in demonstrations and discussed a UGC approach, moderation and eventual licensing. That was an unresolved diligence topic there too. Naming Roblox or Astrocade as precedents does not transfer their permissions or policies to a new platform. The greater emphasis on installed games and local asset reuse makes the range of implementation questions broader for Universal Modder.

This affects the commercial thesis: which titles can attract users, how much installation is required, whether creators can earn, what review costs, and how a publisher relationship could change the product. Clean company ownership does not answer content permissions; permitted content does not establish clean company ownership. [October 14 follow-up] [OpenGame meeting]

Assumptions and formula

Initial ownership = check ÷ post-money basis. Final ownership = initial ownership × (1 − later dilution). Gross proceeds = final ownership × exit equity value. Multiple = proceeds ÷ check. This uses the same dilution and outcome for both, not a forecast that they will be equal.

Actual SAFE conversion and ownership depend on the document and capitalization. Post-money SAFE ownership is measured before subsequent new financing; later rounds and option pools can dilute it. Preferences, fees, taxes, timing, liquidity and instrument differences are omitted. See YC’s SAFE materials. This is an arithmetic sensitivity, not a check recommendation or return prediction.

The default example is deliberately sobering: a $1B outcome with 50% later dilution produces about 6.25× gross at $80M, versus 27.78× at $18M. A $100M outcome under the same dilution does not return the UM check. A $5B outcome is a very different story. The task is to decide whether the team has a plausible path to a sufficiently large outcome, not to assume one because the category is exciting.

A $78M cap instead of $80M improves simple ownership by only about 2.6%. The more important issues are what the company owns, which repeated behavior it wins, how it captures value and whether the initial price leaves room for the risk. No financing scarcity or missed allocation changes those questions.

12 / Turn the analysis into a decision

What would change my mind?

The strongest bull case: capable makers keep their preferred agents, use Universal Modder to turn experiments into reliable shared play, and stay because publishing, multiplayer and feedback are meaningfully better. Mods seed demand; a broader catalogue brings repeat discovery; original or appropriately permitted content expands the opportunity. The team’s speed and practical infrastructure experience compound into a service and, eventually, a network.

The strongest failure case: the team makes outstanding clips that are more fun to watch than to use. Independent creators need ongoing help, installation remains fragile, players leave after the surprise, and the best makers use the open knowledge while distributing elsewhere. The company carries support and content costs without recurring value capture. It could still produce useful tooling or good individual games—and be a poor investment at this price.

Watch a maker

Use an independent, agent-native creator with a real project. Observe publication, a revision and a later return using normal docs. Compare with their existing workflow. Count founder intervention and ask what they would actually miss.

Watch a player

Use a clean machine. Discover, launch, invite a friend, reconnect and return later. Separate original browser games from installed mods and pass-throughs. Record where interest turns into friction.

Inspect cohorts

Separate founders from independent makers; views from successful launches; new from returning players. Look for a second publication, an improvement after feedback, and a player trying a second creator.

Stress-test the appeal

Repeat a slice with original or clearly permitted content. If demand disappears without a familiar franchise, the opportunity depends more heavily on publisher relationships and the mod lane.

Trace the economics

Map creation, failed iterations, builds, storage, multiplayer, moderation and support. Identify who pays and why. Test a service creators value even before the platform has a large audience.

Inspect what is investable

Get the actual entity, capitalization, asset list, assignments/release and financing instrument. Review format-specific content rules separately. Confirm which existing work and relationships travel into the company.

No invented retention threshold can replace understanding these early workflows. We can invest before mature metrics if we explicitly believe the team and opportunity justify it. But the missing evidence should remain visible, and the decision should say which uncertainty we are choosing to bear.

My recommendation today: continue a concentrated diligence process around independent creation, reliable shared play, asset title and the actual terms. Universal Modder’s most promising initial product is the practical home for work made with agents. The public evidence does not yet establish the audience business that could support a much larger outcome.

OpenGame’s lesson is to take the new creative behavior seriously. The missed allocation should neither disqualify Universal Modder nor grant it inherited conviction. The decision is whether this company, at this price, can turn that behavior into durable value.
